# Stage 3 — Generation

**Gate: 150+ raw candidates, all four name types represented, mechanic and source word
recorded next to every one.**

Volume is the whole game and it is where amateur naming loses. Catchword generates ~2,000
per project and shortlists 10%. Lexicon targets 1,000+ ideas before narrowing to 50–100.
A twenty-name list is a brainstorm; it will lose to whatever the founder already had in
their head, because nothing in it is surprising enough to displace an incumbent idea.

**Record the source word and the mechanic next to every candidate.** When you present, the
derivation is most of the argument — a name whose provenance you can state survives a
committee; a name that just appeared does not.

---

## Build the project vocabulary first

Before generating names, build the raw material. An hour here is worth three later.

1. **Synonym sweep.** For each territory, pull synonyms, near-synonyms and antonyms. Power
   Thesaurus and Wordnik surface usage that a standard thesaurus misses.
2. **Translate.** Take the ten strongest roots into the target-market languages, plus Latin,
   Greek, and one language chosen for sound rather than meaning (Italian and Finnish are
   both productive for vowel-rich, sayable roots).
3. **Etymologies.** Follow the roots back. The Proto-Indo-European ancestor of a worn-out
   English word is frequently an unclaimed, sayable, ownable syllable.
4. **Domain jargon.** The words practitioners use that outsiders do not. From brief Q6.
5. **Adjacent fields.** If the territory is mining, also raid geology, metallurgy, assaying,
   surveying. The neighbouring vocabulary is less picked over.

Target: **200–400 root words** before a single candidate is constructed.

---

## Parallel teams with different briefs

Lexicon does not brainstorm in one big room. It runs small teams, and not all of them get the
real brief. For Microsoft's 2006 media player, Placek ran three teams: one briefed on the
real project, one told it was naming a hypothetical Sony player, and one briefed as if for a
different client. He has never said which team produced *Zune*. A team that does not know
the client cannot defer to the client's taste, the house style, or the founder's favourite.

Agents make this cheap. Run **four isolated generators in parallel** (sub-agents where the
harness has them; otherwise sequential passes in fresh contexts that never see each other's
output) and merge only at the end:

| Team | Brief it receives | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Straight** | The real brief, the namescape, all territories | The professional baseline |
| **B — Decoy** | The same job, displaced: a different company, a different country, or a different category with the same emotional job (naming a *ferry* service when the product moves data) | Breaks category vocabulary |
| **C — Forge** | Only the Forge territories from `03-name-types.md`, no competitor list | The bold option; keeps the off-strategy slot honest |
| **D — Cold** | One line: what it does and for whom. No territories, no namescape, no constraints except form | The control. If D beats A, the brief or the territories are wrong |

Rules:

- **Isolation is the method.** Teams never see each other's lists until the merge. A team
  that has read team A's list is just team A again.
- **Each team owes its share of the volume** (≈40+ raw each, so the merged pool clears 150)
  and records mechanic + source word for every candidate, as below.
- **Decoy briefs are translated back at the merge.** A ferry name is judged as a name for
  the real product; most will die, and the few that survive are usually the best surprises.
- **Tag every candidate with its team** and keep the tag all the way to the report. If the
  shortlist is all team A, say so — the decoy teams did not earn their cost on this project.
- **Record which models actually ran.** "Four teams" run by one model in one context is one
  team. If parallel agents were unavailable, write that in the report.

The merge is the only place duplicates are removed. Do not ask the teams to filter.

## The mechanics

Organise candidates **by mechanic, not by theme**. Theme grouping hides the fact that half
the list is the same word four times; mechanic grouping shows the shape of the search and
makes gaps visible.

### Real-word and near-word mechanics

1. **Arbitrary lift** — a real word from an unrelated field, used for the resonance.
   `apple`, `oracle`, `patagonia`
2. **Foreign lift** — the same, from another language. Check the trap register hard.
   `volvo` (Latin, "I roll") · `nike` (Greek)
3. **Archaic revival** — a real word that has fallen out of use, so the domain may live.
   `fathom`, `augur`, `winnow`
4. **Compound** — two real words fused. Cheapest to understand, hardest to own.
   `blackboard`, `salesforce`
5. **Verbing** — a name that is already an instruction. Strong for tools.
   `stripe`, `slack`, `notion`

### Coinage mechanics

6. **Suffix-add** — root + `a` / `o` / `ia` / `io` / `ly` / `oo` / `ra`.
   `sift → sifta` · `deliver → deliveroo`
7. **Truncate + vowel** — cut to the sayable core, land on a vowel.
   `skarbiec → karbia` · `namierzyć → namira`
8. **Consonant swap / softening** — remove the cluster the second market will not attempt.
   `skarb → skarvo` · `trzygłów → trygla`
9. **Portmanteau** — two roots fused, both still audible.
   `pin + interest → pinterest` · `micro + soft`
10. **Vowel-shift** — change one vowel to internationalise the sound.
    `rokita → ritka`
11. **Morpheme assembly** — build from Greek/Latin parts with intended connotations.
    Lexicon's method: a matrix of ~55 prefixes × ~102 suffixes, filtered by sound. Cold on
    its own; excellent as raw material for mechanics 7 and 8.
12. **Prefix graft** — a meaning-bearing prefix on a neutral stem.
    `veri-`, `alti-`, `omni-`, `sub-`, `trans-`
13. **Misspelling** — a real word respelled to become ownable. Costs you the radio test
    every single time; use sparingly and score the spelling penalty honestly.
    `flickr`, `froot loops`
14. **Pure sound** — built from phonemes for their connotations, meaning attached
    afterwards. See `05-sound-symbolism.md`. This is how *Sonos*, *Vercel* and *Zappos*
    happen, and it is the mechanic most people never try.

### The matrix trick

For mechanics 11 and 12, build an actual grid: prefixes down, suffixes across, and read the
diagonals. It is mechanical, it is fast, and it produces the unexpected combinations that
free-association cannot reach. Most cells are garbage. That is fine — you need 150 to keep
40, and the grid is where the volume comes from cheaply.

## Blue-sky pass

After the systematic pass, one deliberately unsystematic one. Ten minutes, no filtering:

- pair the most obvious category word with the least obvious modifier
- name it after the founder's obsession, the office street, the failure mode it prevents
- ask what a competitor would never dare call it
- write the five names that are *too much*, then pull each back one notch

The point is not that these survive. It is that they widen the range, and the pulled-back
version of a too-much name often lands exactly where an evocative name needs to be.

---

## Form constraints — apply during generation, not after

These are cheap to satisfy while writing and expensive to retrofit.

- **Plain ASCII. No diacritics at all.** A name the second market cannot type is not a name.
- **2–3 syllables, 4–10 characters.** Vercel is six letters. Klarna is six.
- **Not a real word** in either target language — recognisably derived, not lifted (this
  applies to the coinage territories; the real-word territories are exempt by definition,
  and get scrutinised harder at clearance instead).
- **No consonant clusters the second market will not attempt** — `szcz`, `trz`, initial `zd`.
- **One obvious spelling from the sound, one obvious sound from the spelling.** If both
  directions are not unambiguous, the name leaks customers at every word-of-mouth handoff.
- **It must read as software** (Klarna, Revolut, Miro, Xero, Canva), not as folklore — unless
  the brief says otherwise.

## Volume checkpoints

| Checkpoint | Count | If you are short |
|---|---|---|
| Root vocabulary | 200–400 | Go back to etymologies and adjacent fields |
| Raw candidates | 150+, from all four teams | Run the matrix on two more territories; check a team didn't quietly under-deliver |
| Passing form screen | 40+ | The form constraints are being applied too early |
| Scored | 8–15 | Loosen the screen, do not lower the scorecard |
| Presented | 3–5 | — |

**If a stage comes up short, say so in the output.** A funnel that ran at a third of depth
produces a real answer; a funnel that *claims* to have run at full depth and did not is how
a mediocre name gets an unearned mandate.
