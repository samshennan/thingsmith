# Stage 0 — The brief

**Gate: the name's job is written down in one sentence, and the principal has seen it.**

Without this, every later argument is taste. With it, "I don't like it" becomes "it doesn't
do the job", which is answerable.

Do not spend a session on this. Twenty minutes, or one round of questions. If the principal
is not available, write the brief from what you know, mark the assumptions, and put them at
the top of the final document where they can be corrected.

---

## The four strategic questions

Lexicon opens every engagement with these. Answer them before anything else.

1. **What does winning look like?** Not "a good name" — the actual commercial outcome.
2. **What do we have to win?** The one thing that must go right.
3. **What do we need to win?** What the name specifically has to contribute, as distinct
   from the logo, the product, the price, the deck.
4. **What do we need to say?** The single message. If there are three, there is no brief yet.

Question 3 is the one that gets skipped and the one that matters. A name cannot carry
positioning, category education, a feature list and a promise at once. Decide which job is
the name's and which belongs to the tagline, the deck, or the first sentence of the site.

---

## The intake questions

Adapted from Catchword's creative brief. Ask what is unknown; do not interrogate what is
already in the room.

**The thing**
1. What exactly is being named — company, product, feature, service line, event?
2. Where does it sit in the existing brand architecture? Standalone, sub-brand, endorsed,
   or part of a naming system that already has rules?
3. What is it, in one sentence a stranger would understand?
4. What does it replace or displace?

**The audience**
5. Who buys it? Who *uses* it? (Often different, and the name usually serves the buyer.)
6. What do they call the problem today, in their own words?
7. How will they first encounter the name — spoken, in a search result, on a slide, on an
   invoice, in a repo?

**The position**
8. What is the one differentiator?
9. What are the three adjectives, and — harder — the three the brand is explicitly *not*?
10. What conversation in this category could the name change or own?

**The constraints**
11. Which markets, in which order? Which languages must the name survive?
12. Is an exact-match `.com` a requirement, a preference, or irrelevant?
13. Is there a house style — a pantheon, a place, a field — the name should sit inside?
14. What is the budget for a domain acquisition, if the right name is parked?
15. Who signs off, and what will they personally object to? (Ask this one out loud. It
    predicts the death of more shortlists than any linguistic factor.)

---

## The market pair

State it explicitly, in order: **home market now, second market later.**

The default for this workspace is **Poland now, Australia/UK later**, but say it every time
rather than assuming — an EU-only name and a PL/AU name are different projects, and the
second market is a veto stage later, so it has to be named now.

For each market record:
- the language(s) the name will be read in
- whether the market reads Latin script natively
- the local TLD, and whether it has an eligibility rule (`.com.au` requires an Australian
  entity or ABN — it cannot be bought from abroad)
- the national trademark register that will need searching

---

## House style — and the escape hatch

If the parent brand has a naming universe, the new name should be able to sit in it. But
**never at the cost of the second market's pronunciation**, and never as the only option
explored.

Always generate one territory that abandons the house style entirely, so the choice the
principal makes is a real one. If house style wins, it wins against a live alternative
rather than by default.

---

## Output of this stage

Four lines, in the final document, before anything else:

```
Naming:        <the thing>
Name's job:    <one sentence — what this name must do that nothing else can>
Markets:       <home> now, <second> later
Hard constraints: <TLD requirement / house style / architecture / anything non-negotiable>
```

Anything assumed rather than confirmed is marked `(assumed)` and repeated in the final
document. Assumptions that survive to the decision are the ones that blow up afterwards.
