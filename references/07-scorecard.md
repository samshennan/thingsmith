# Stage 5 — The scorecard

**Gate: every survivor scored on the same ten dimensions; 8–15 carried into verification.**

The scorecard exists to convert "I like it" into something arguable. Igor's framing is the
right one: subjective personal responses — *I like it*, *it reminds me of an old
girlfriend* — are exactly that, and have no bearing on whether a name will work in the
market. But a client is entitled to ask what *does* determine that, and a number they can
see the reasoning behind is the answer.

Score **up to 10 per dimension, 100 maximum.** Nine of the ten are Igor's original
categories, proven over decades of client work; the tenth was added because it kills more
names in practice than any of the other nine.

---

## The ten dimensions

| # | Dimension | The question | Weight note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Positioning** | How well does it support the *specific* positioning in the brief, and how many relevant messages does it map to? | The dimension a name cannot be good at by accident |
| 2 | **Distinctive** | How differentiated from the competitive set on the stage-1 grid? Judge it *in context*, against the field it will spar with — never in isolation. | Prerequisite for memorability |
| 3 | **Depth** | Layers of meaning and association. Great names never reveal everything at once; they keep offering new ideas. | Igor's "deep well"; Watkins' "legs" |
| 4 | **Energy** | Is it vital, alive, buzzy? Could it carry an ad campaign on its shoulders? | Where tame names die |
| 5 | **Humanity** | Warmth versus clinical coldness. Test: could it be a nickname for one of your children? | Where Greek/Latin coinages score 2 |
| 6 | **Sound** | Both how it sounds *and* how easily a customer says it. If people are not comfortable saying it, word of mouth never starts. | Cross-check against `05-sound-symbolism.md` |
| 7 | **Appearance** | How it looks as a visual signifier — in a logo, a URL, a slide, an app icon. Letterforms, symmetry, ascenders, length. | Always seen in context, but always seen |
| 8 | **"33"** | The brand-magic score: does it make someone lean forward and want to know more, and want to tell someone else? Named for the unexplained "33" on Rolling Rock bottles that everyone talks about because nobody knows what it means. | The hardest to fake and the best predictor of free PR |
| 9 | **Trademark** | Not subjective. **10** = likely available · **5** = may be available · **0** = not likely available (remove from consideration). | Fill from stage 6, not from a guess |
| 10 | **Reach** | Can it actually be had? `.com` status, home-market TLD, handle bundle, and the real cost of acquiring what is parked. **0** if the name cannot be assembled at any price the brief allows. | The addition to Igor's nine — see below |

### Why "Reach" is a dimension and not a footnote

Igor's nine were written for clients who could buy a domain out of petty cash. For a small
company, an unobtainable name is not a good name with a problem — it is not a name. Scoring
reach as a dimension keeps availability *inside* the decision instead of turning it into a
veto applied afterwards, which is what produces the familiar death spiral of a
much-loved shortlist collapsing one candidate at a time.

Suggested anchors:

| Score | Situation |
|---|---|
| 10 | Exact-match `.com` free, home TLD free, handle bundle essentially clean |
| 7–8 | `.com` free, one or two significant handles taken |
| 5–6 | `.com` parked at a knowable four-figure price the brief can absorb |
| 3–4 | `.com` gone; a credible alternative TLD strategy exists (`.ai`, `.io`, home TLD) |
| 1–2 | Only awkward modifier domains (`get-`, `try-`, `-app`) remain |
| 0 | Cannot be assembled at any price the brief allows |

## Running it

**Score the competitors too.** Put three or four names from the stage-1 grid — including the
incumbent being displaced — through the same card. Without them there is no scale, and a
candidate scoring 61 means nothing. When the market leader scores 48 and a candidate scores
71, the conversation changes completely.

**Score fast, one line of comment per cell, and do not agonise.** The value is in the
comparison and in the *pattern*, not the arithmetic. Rarely will a name top every category;
the best names score consistently well across all of them, and a name with three 9s and four
3s is usually worse than one with straight 7s.

**Watch for the shape, not just the total:**

- High Positioning + low Distinctive → right idea, crowded execution. Regenerate in the
  same territory with a different mechanic.
- High Energy + low Trademark → the classic heartbreak. Check the trademark column *before*
  anyone reads the name aloud in a meeting.
- High everything + low Humanity → a Greek/Latin coinage. Fine if the brief wants a neutral
  vessel, fatal if it wants warmth.
- Low "33" across the entire shortlist → generation was too safe. Go back to stage 3 and run
  the blue-sky pass properly.

**Weighting.** Straight sum is the default and is usually right. If the brief demands a
weighting, set it *before* scoring and write it down — 30% strategic fit, 25% distinctiveness,
20% memorability, 15% legal is a common commercial split. Setting weights after seeing the
scores is just picking the favourite with extra steps.

## The one thing the scorecard does not do

It does not choose. A total is an argument, not a verdict, and a name that scores 68 with a
story the principal can tell beats a 74 they cannot. Present the numbers, present the shape,
and leave the decision where it belongs.
