# Stage 2 — Name types and territories

**Gate: 4+ territories chosen, spanning all four name types, at least one off-strategy.**

Two independent classifications matter. **Approach** is what the name does to the reader.
**Construct** is how the word is built. They are orthogonal — an evocative name can be a
real word (Apple), a compound (Blackboard) or a coinage (Yahoo).

---

## The four approaches

Igor's taxonomy, which is the clearest of the several in circulation, with the trade-offs
each carries.

### 1. Functional / descriptive
Explains what the thing does. *Infoseek, Salesforce, The Naming Company.*

- **Works** as a *product* name under a strong company brand, where the company name carries
  the equity: BMW 3 Series, Microsoft Teams, Subway.
- **Fails** as a company name. Descriptive names are drawn from a small pool of relevant
  keywords, so competitors converge on the same words and fade into each other. Igor's
  demonstration of this is a list of twenty-eight naming firms called Name Development, Name
  Evolution, Name Generator, Name Lab, Name One, Name Quest, Name Works, The Naming Company.
  Not one is memorable, and each paid for the privilege.
- **Legal**: weakest end of the distinctiveness spectrum. Descriptive marks need acquired
  secondary meaning to register at all; generic terms never register.
- **The counter-argument that kills it**: a company name never appears without context. It
  is on a site, a card, a slide, in a sentence. It does not need to explain the business,
  which frees it to do something more valuable.

### 2. Experiential
Connects to a real human experience of the thing. *Navigator, Explorer, Safari, Magellan.*

- **Works** for early entrants in a category, and where corporate approval is hard —
  experiential names are intuitive so they pass committees easily.
- **Fails** on differentiation. Explorer, Navigator, Safari and Magellan are all saying
  exactly the same thing to exactly the same people. They are also all SUVs. Intuitive names
  get used across many industries, which makes them harder to trademark and less effective
  the later you arrive.

### 3. Evocative
Evokes the *positioning* rather than the function or the experience. *Apple, Virgin, Yahoo,
Oracle, Amazon, Patagonia.*

- **Works** as the strongest differentiator available. Rare in most categories, nonlinear,
  multidimensional, and capable of building a brand bigger than the goods.
- **Fails** when created out of sync with positioning, in which case it is just a random
  word — and fails in the approval meeting more often than in the market. Expect this
  feedback, because Igor documented every one of these being said about names that went on
  to win: *Virgin* — "says we're new at this, investors won't take us seriously". *Oracle* —
  "unreliable, only foretold death, sounds like orifice". *The Gap* — "means something is
  missing". *Caterpillar* — "creepy-crawly, easy to squash". *Banana Republic* — "you'll be
  picketed".
- **The defence**: the public does not evaluate names this way; internal committees do.
  Evaluate an evocative name against the positioning, never against free association. Have
  the four qualities ready as the argument: does it self-propel, connect emotionally, carry
  personality, and offer a deep well of imagery?

### 4. Invented / coined
Two very different sub-types, and conflating them is a common error.

**4a. Greek/Latin morpheme constructions.** *Agilent, Aquent, Accenture, Alliant.*
Breeze through trademark, easy domains, no negative connotations, serious-sounding, and the
easiest thing to get approved at a large corporation. But they are emotionally void and
image-free — you need a global advertising budget to give them meaning. Choose this only if
you have that budget or genuinely need a neutral vessel.

**4b. Poetically constructed names** built on rhythm and the pleasure of saying them.
*Google, Oreo, Snapple, Kleenex, Klarna, Revolut.*
Same trademark and domain advantages, plus memorability, emotional engagement and word of
mouth — the target audience *likes saying them*, which propels the name for free. Harder to
get approved, because "fun to say, memorable, viral" needs a quantified case. That case is
the scorecard in stage 5.

## The construct axis

| Construct | How | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Real word** | Lifted whole, used arbitrarily or metaphorically | Apple, Coach, Amazon, Dove |
| **Compound** | Two real words fused | Facebook, Blackboard, Salesforce, BestBuy |
| **Lexical** | Wordplay — pun, misspelling, alliteration, onomatopoeia | Froot Loops, Flickr, Dunkin' |
| **Portmanteau** | Two roots fused, both still audible | Pinterest, Instagram, Microsoft |
| **Truncation** | Cut to the sayable core | Cisco, Fedex, Intel |
| **Foreign / classical** | A word from another language or a dead one | Volvo, Nike, Audi, Lego |
| **Coined** | Built from morphemes or from sound alone | Kodak, Xerox, Häagen-Dazs, Vercel |
| **Acronym / alphanumeric** | Initials or codes | IBM, UX 200 |
| **Eponymous / geographic** | A person or a place | Ford, Patagonia, Fuji |

Acronyms and alphanumerics are near-always wrong for a new brand: no meaning, no imagery,
no sound, and they force the market to memorise arbitrary letters. Include one or two only
as a control.

## Trademark strength runs alongside, not parallel

The USPTO distinctiveness spectrum is a *different* axis from the four approaches, and the
mapping is loose but useful when arguing for an unexpected name:

| Spectrum | Registrable? | Typically corresponds to |
|---|---|---|
| **Fanciful** (invented, no prior meaning) | Strongest; inherently distinctive | Invented 4a/4b — Kodak, Exxon, Xerox |
| **Arbitrary** (real word, unrelated to the goods) | Strong; inherently distinctive | Evocative real-word — Apple for computers |
| **Suggestive** (needs a leap of imagination) | Inherently distinctive | Experiential and some evocative — Netflix |
| **Descriptive** | Only with acquired secondary meaning | Functional — "The Cupcake Shoppe" |
| **Generic** | Never | Functional at its worst |

So the commercially strongest quadrant and the legally strongest quadrant point the same
way: **away from descriptive.** That alignment is the single most useful argument you have
when a committee wants the safe functional compound.

## Choosing territories

A territory is a *direction*, not a word: a metaphor, a field of reference, a sound world, a
feeling. Choose at least four, and make them genuinely different from each other so that one
bad territory cannot kill the project.

**Compose the set like this:**

| Slot | Requirement |
|---|---|
| 1 | Aims at the empty quadrant from stage 1 |
| 2 | A metaphor with native roots in **both** markets |
| 3 | Pure sound — built from phonetics, not meaning (see `05-sound-symbolism.md`) |
| 4 | Off-strategy — abandons the house style entirely |
| 5+ | Optional: the crowded quadrant done unusually well, as a control |

### Finding the bi-cultural metaphor

The trick, and it usually works: ask what the two markets *have in common*, not what a word
means. Look for a metaphor with native roots on both sides.

> Poland is Silesian coal. Australia is Ballarat gold. English-speaking sales already says
> "prospecting" every day without hearing that it is a mining word. One metaphor, two
> cultures, no translation.

### Mining a territory in three layers

Once you have a territory, diverge inside it across three layers. This generates far more
usable roots than free-association, and each layer has a different tone.

| Layer | Mining | Divination | Cartography |
|---|---|---|---|
| The thing found | lode, seam, vein, paydirt, nugget | the sign, the omen | landmark, bearing, fix |
| The machinery | sluice, cradle, pan, sift, winnow | the rod, the dowser | compass, sextant, astrolabe |
| The person | prospector, assayer, fossicker | augur, diviner, seer | pilot, scout, surveyor |

The *machinery* layer is usually the most productive and the least claimed — everyone names
after the treasure, almost nobody after the sieve.

## Output of this stage

A short table: territory · what it is · which quadrant it aims at · why it works in both
markets. Then converge — pick two or three to generate heavily from, and one to generate
lightly from as a control. Record the ones you dropped and why; that list answers "did you
consider…" later.
