# Sound symbolism — the phonetics layer

Use this twice: as a **generation mechanic** (build the name from the sounds the positioning
needs) and as a **selection filter** (check that a candidate's sound is not fighting its
meaning).

This is not decoration. Lexicon has spent over three million dollars on proprietary sound
symbolism research and uses software built on it to pick letters before words. The academic
base is solid and replicated: Lowrey & Shrum (2007), Yorkston & Menon (2004), and the
broader phonosemantics literature all find that consumers infer product attributes from
phonemes, automatically, without noticing they are doing it.

The single most useful fact: **the effect operates below awareness.** People cannot tell you
why the name felt fast. They just report that it did. So a candidate whose sound contradicts
its positioning loses on a dimension no focus group will name.

---

## Vowels — size, speed, weight

The best-replicated finding in the field.

| | **Front vowels** — i, e, ee, ih (*bit, beet, bet*) | **Back vowels** — o, u, oo, aw (*bought, boot, boat*) |
|---|---|---|
| Size | smaller | larger |
| Weight | lighter | heavier |
| Speed | faster | slower |
| Texture | thinner, sharper | thicker, rounder |
| Temperature | colder | warmer |
| Gender coding | more feminine | more masculine |
| Distance | closer | further |
| Feel | friendlier, prettier, weaker | more powerful, more substantial |

**Use it:** a lightweight fast tool wants front vowels (*Slick, Nimble, Miro, Stripe*). A
heavy, substantial, powerful thing wants back vowels (*Volvo, Ford, Bold, Sonos*). A name
that must feel both premium and light is a genuinely hard brief and usually resolves to a
front vowel with a soft consonant frame.

## Consonants — force, speed, trust

| Class | Sounds | Connotation |
|---|---|---|
| **Plosives / stops** | p, b, t, d, k, g | Impact, power, abruptness, confidence, weight. Perceived as larger and slower. |
| **Fricatives** | f, v, s, z, sh, th | Speed, smoothness, flow, lightness. Perceived as smaller and faster. |
| **Sibilants** | s, z, sh | Speed and slipperiness; too many reads slick in the bad sense. |
| **Nasals** | m, n, ng | Warmth, softness, humanity, comfort. |
| **Liquids** | l, r | Flow, ease, fluidity. `l` is gentle; `r` is more assertive. |

Lexicon's proprietary findings, which are consistent with the published research and worth
knowing because they are what a naming professional will say in the room:

- **`v` reads most alive and vibrant** — Corvette, Viagra, Vercel, Volvo
- **`b` reads most reliable** — BlackBerry, Bosch
- **`z` draws attention** — Azure, Zappos, Zara
- **`x` signals innovation and technology** — Xerox, Xbox, Exxon
- **`k` reads crisp, precise, engineered** — Kodak, Klarna, Nikon

---

## Structural properties

- **Initial plosive** (`k`, `t`, `p`, `b`, `g`, `d`) gives a name a hard attack — it cuts
  through in speech and is easier to hear on a phone. A disproportionate share of memorable
  brands start with one.
- **Ending on a vowel** softens and internationalises: *Canva, Miro, Klarna, Sifta, Karbia.*
  It also makes the name inflect gracefully in Slavic languages, which matters for the
  Polish market specifically.
- **Ending on a hard consonant** feels finished, technical, decisive: *Vercel, Stripe, Slack.*
- **Repeated vowel** — *Google, Kodak, Zappos* — is disproportionately memorable and fun to
  say. Igor's whole argument for poetically constructed names lives here.
- **Two syllables beats three.** Three beats four. Nothing beats "easy to say twice."
- **`-ium`, `-ex`, `-tech`, `-tron`** read scientific because of the words they echo (sodium,
  uranium). Use deliberately or not at all — accidentally scientific is a real failure mode
  for a warm brand.

## The generation move

Do this properly at least once per project; it is mechanic 14 in `04-generation.md`.

1. From the brief, write the three adjectives the name must project.
2. Translate each into its phonetic requirements using the tables above.
   *"fast, light, precise"* → front vowels, fricatives or a `k`, two syllables, hard attack.
   *"substantial, trustworthy, permanent"* → back vowels, `b` or `d`, nasals, three syllables.
3. Build twenty candidates that satisfy the phonetic spec, **with no meaning at all**.
4. Then look for meaning to attach — a root that nearly matches one of the twenty, a
   territory word that can be bent toward it.

This runs the process backwards from how everyone does it, and it is the reliable route to
names that *feel* right before they *mean* anything. Sonos, Vercel and Zappos were all found
this way.

---

## The selection filter

For each shortlisted candidate, one line:

> `karbia` — back-ish `a` frame, plosive `k` attack, `b` reliability, ends on a vowel.
> Reads substantial and trustworthy rather than fast. **Fits** a records/archive positioning;
> **fights** a real-time-alerts positioning.

If a name's sound fights its positioning, that is a scorecard deduction on *Positioning* and
*Sound* both, and it should be said out loud rather than felt vaguely.

---

## Caveats worth stating in the report

- These are population-level tendencies, not laws. They shift the odds, they do not decide.
- Most of the published research is on English speakers. The vowel size/weight effects
  replicate broadly and are close to universal; consonant connotations are more
  language-bound and should be sanity-checked against the second market.
- Sound symbolism never rescues a name that fails the four-language test or the trap
  register. It is a tiebreaker and a generator, not a defence.
