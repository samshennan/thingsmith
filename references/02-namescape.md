# Stage 1 — The competitive namescape

**Gate: every meaningful competitor plotted on the grid, and the empty quadrant named.**

This is the highest-leverage twenty minutes in the whole process and the stage amateurs
skip. It is step one at Igor, step one at Zinzin, and the thing Lexicon means by "unexpected
within the category". It tells you which *kind* of name to aim for before you write one.

The insight it produces is always the same shape: **the category's names are clustered, and
the cluster is the opportunity.** When Apple was named, the computer industry was IBM, NEC,
DEC, ADPAC, Cincom, Sperry Rand, Syncsort, Tesseract. The empty quadrant was warm, human,
simple — and a fruit walked into it unopposed.

---

## Build the list

15–30 names. Include:

- direct competitors, including the incumbent being displaced
- the adjacent category the buyer might use instead
- the two or three category leaders the buyer already respects, even if not competitors
- anyone who has *taken the obvious metaphor* — this is where the collision search starts
  early and cheap

Search the way the buyer would: the category term, the problem in the buyer's own words
(from brief Q6), and the tender/procurement language if the buyer is institutional.

---

## Plot the grid

Two axes. Vertical is **approach** (what the name does), horizontal is **construct** (how
the name is built).

```
                  REAL WORD        COMPOUND          COINED
FUNCTIONAL     |  Salesforce   |  BestBuy       |  Infoseek      |
EXPERIENTIAL   |  Navigator    |  Freshbooks    |  Netscape      |
EVOCATIVE      |  Apple        |  Blackboard    |  Yahoo         |
ABSTRACT       |  Coach        |  --            |  Kodak, Xerox  |
```

Put every competitor in a cell. Then look at what is empty.

Three things fall out immediately:

1. **The crowded cell** — where most competitors sit. A name here starts at a disadvantage
   no budget fixes. It is not forbidden, but it must earn its place on the scorecard's
   *distinctive* row against a stacked field.
2. **The empty cell** — usually one or two. This is the territory brief. Most B2B software
   categories are jammed into FUNCTIONAL/COMPOUND and leave EVOCATIVE wide open.
3. **The shared metaphor** — if three competitors are all mining, or all navigating, or all
   building, the metaphor is claimed. Note it now; it saves killing four candidates later.

---

## Score the field's tone

For each competitor name, one word on what it projects: *technical, safe, corporate, warm,
playful, clinical, aggressive, premium, generic.* Count them.

If eleven of fifteen read "technical, safe, corporate", that is the finding. Write it as a
sentence, because it is the sentence that justifies an unexpected name to a nervous
committee:

> "Fourteen of the sixteen names in this category are functional compounds that read
> corporate and interchangeable. A buyer cannot distinguish them on a shortlist. The
> opportunity is the evocative quadrant, which is empty."

---

## Search-engine namescape

Separate check, five minutes, and it kills candidates before they cost anything.

For the two or three obvious metaphors the category suggests, search the metaphor plus the
category term. You are looking for:

- someone already trading under it in either market
- a software package, library, or model that owns the word among the exact people you are
  selling to
- a listed company or a nationally known person holding the word in the home market

A real kill from this workspace: `fossick` — an Australian gold-rush word for scavenging,
perfect for a prospecting metaphor. A subagent reported it clean. A second search found an
Australian recruiter using the identical metaphor in the same market. The metaphor was
claimed, not just the word.

---

## Output of this stage

Three things, carried into the final document:

1. The grid, with competitors in cells.
2. One sentence naming the crowded cell and the empty one.
3. A list of metaphors already claimed in-market, with who holds them.

The territory brief for stage 2 is: **aim at the empty quadrant, and avoid the claimed
metaphors.** If the principal's instinct is the crowded cell, that is now an explicit,
argued choice rather than an accident.
